Microgravity results reach Earth through models

A flame near 2,300 K sits in air near 300 K. That is an eight-fold density change across about a centimetre, and on Earth it drives a rising flow that hides the chemistry. A space result does not apply to Earth directly. It goes through three steps.

  1. Step 1Remove buoyancy

    In orbit or a drop tower the rising flow disappears. The flame becomes close to one-dimensional, so temperature, soot and extinction can be measured cleanly.

  2. Step 2Test the model

    A combustion model is checked against that simple flame. If it fails here, it has no chance on a real fire.

  3. Step 3Add Earth back

    The validated model is extended with buoyancy, wind and turbulence, then checked again against 1 g experiments before anyone relies on it.

Fire safety basics for Earth

A fire needs fuel, oxygen and heat, and removing any one controls it. Each experiment family works on one side of that triangle.

Take one side of the triangle away

A candle burns because fuel, oxygen and heat are all present. Remove any one and it goes out.

Principle from NASA Glenn's Beginner's Guide to combustion. Animation is illustrative.

Fuel

BASS, BASS-II and Saffire test which materials ignite and spread flame, so the safest can be chosen.

Oxygen

FLEX and BASS measure how well CO2, helium, SF6 and nitrogen starve a flame.

Heat

A flame that loses heat faster than it makes it goes out. Flame Design tests the hypothesis that spherical flames die once their peak falls below 1,130 K.

Directions the flame experiments support. They are design principles to test on your own burner, not operating settings.

    Where this stopsThe benchmark flames are small and laminar. Industrial burners are turbulent and momentum-driven, and soot behaves differently at 0 g and 1 g, so soot amounts cannot be copied across. Only a model validated at both can carry the result.

    Who knows what in this scenario
    Wind, slope and moisture effects come from field and laboratory fire science on Earth.
    Whether the fuel ignites, keeps burning or goes out at a given oxygen level and airflow is the kind of physics BASS-II measures and uses to test flame-spread models.
    What the fire emits is measured from aircraft and satellites. NASA Langley flew 12 campaigns over six ecosystems from 1986 to 1993 and built an emissions model based on fire temperature.
    Where this stopsThis is the weakest link on the page. One record, BASS-II, says its validated models can be used to study wildfires and building fires. A wildfire adds wind, terrain, vegetation and turbulence at scales no chamber reaches, and no file here contains wildfire measurements.

    Soot and air pollution

    Combustion supplies about 85% of the energy delivered in the United States and burning fossil fuels is the main cause of current climate change. The harm comes from what a flame gives off. Microgravity research speaks to some of these products and not to others.

    Engine efficiency scenario

    Raise the compression ratio or run leaner and an engine turns more of its fuel into work. Both moves run into limits set by low-temperature flame chemistry, which is what the cool-flame experiments measure.

    Where the NASA research comes in
    Knock is fuel igniting on its own through low-temperature chemistry. The Cool Flames record says this chemistry is what sets a fuel's octane and cetane rating.
    The ACME CFI-G record says real engines reach 38% or lower and that ultra-lean combustion could approach 60%, but poor understanding of cool-flame chemistry is holding that back.
    Where this stopsThe experiments are single droplets and laminar gas flames at about 0.25 to 5 atm. Real engines run turbulent at 20 to 40 atm. The link is through chemistry models, not through engine tests.
